What is the cloud?

When you save a file to the desktop of a school computer, it is saved locally — on that specific machine. If you sit at a different computer tomorrow, your file is gone.

When you save to the cloud, your file lives on a server you can reach from any device with an internet connection. Your school Office 365 account gives you 1TB of cloud storage through OneDrive — roughly 500,000 photos worth of space.

Key idea: The cloud is not a mysterious place. It is just someone else's computer — a very large, secure one — that you access over the internet.
Three things that go wrong without cloud saving
  • You finish coursework on a school computer, go home, and cannot access it
  • A computer crashes and you lose hours of work with no backup
  • You email yourself files and end up with five versions called "final", "final2", "ACTUAL final"

All three are solved by saving your work to OneDrive from the start.

Getting started with OneDrive

OneDrive is Microsoft's cloud storage service, built into your school Office 365 account.

  • Go to office.com and sign in with your school email and password
  • Click the grid icon (the "waffle") in the top-left corner
  • Select OneDrive from the apps list
Tip: You can also access OneDrive directly from Word, PowerPoint, or Excel when you click Save. Look for your school name under the save locations.
AutoSave and Version History

When a file is stored in OneDrive, the AutoSave toggle appears in the top-left of Word, Excel, and PowerPoint. When it is on, every change is saved automatically every few seconds.

Version History keeps a full record of every version of your file. If you accidentally delete something or want to see an earlier draft, you can recover it: File → Version History.

AutoSave only works when the file is in OneDrive or SharePoint — not on a desktop or USB drive.

Why file attachments create chaos

Person A writes a draft and emails it to Person B and Person C. They both edit their own copies. Now there are three different versions and nobody knows which is the real document.

This is called version conflict. Real-time collaboration tools solve it entirely — when everyone edits the same cloud file, there is only ever one version.

How to share a file in OneDrive
  • Right-click the file in OneDrive and choose Share
  • Type the email address of the person you want to share with
  • Choose their permission level: Can edit or Can view
  • Add a message if you like, then click Send
Permissions matter: "Can edit" lets someone change the document. "Can view" means they can read it but not change anything. Choose carefully — especially for coursework.
Co-authoring and Comments

Once a document is shared and both people have it open, you can see each other's changes live. Each person's cursor appears in a different colour with their name next to it.

  • Use Comments (Insert → Comment) to suggest changes without editing the main text
  • Use Track Changes when you want edits to be visible and approved individually
  • Check Version History to see who changed what and when

The Office 365 toolkit

Your school account includes a full suite of tools. The challenge is knowing which one to reach for.

  • Word — long-form writing, essays, reports, letters
  • PowerPoint — presentations, visual explanations, slide-based content
  • Excel — data, calculations, charts, tracking information
  • OneNote — notes, research, revision, anything you need to organise and search
  • Forms — surveys, quizzes, data collection from other people
  • Teams — communication, group work, sharing files with a class or group
Why tool choice matters

Using the wrong tool makes the work harder and the output worse. A few common mistakes:

  • Writing a long essay in PowerPoint — the format fights the content
  • Using Word for a data table that needs sorting and filtering — Excel does this far better
  • Emailing individual files back and forth instead of using Teams or OneDrive
  • Taking research notes in Word instead of OneNote — no easy tagging, searching, or organisation
Key question to ask yourself: What does this piece of work actually need — writing, data, visuals, or collaboration? Start from the need, then pick the tool.
OneNote — the underused one

OneNote is probably the most underused app in the Office 365 suite. It is a digital notebook that lets you:

  • Organise notes into notebooks, sections, and pages
  • Search across everything you have ever written instantly
  • Add images, links, tables, and drawings alongside text
  • Sync across all your devices automatically

Many students find it transforms how they take and organise notes for revision.

What AI tools actually are

Tools like ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini are called large language models (LLMs). They have been trained on enormous amounts of text from the internet and other sources. They predict what word is most likely to come next in a sentence — over and over again — to generate responses that sound fluent and confident.

Key idea: AI does not know things the way you know things. It does not have understanding, opinions, or memory of your previous conversations (unless it is specifically built to). It generates plausible-sounding text based on patterns in its training data.
What AI is genuinely good at
  • Explaining concepts in different ways until one clicks
  • Summarising long texts quickly
  • Generating first drafts of writing to react to and improve
  • Brainstorming ideas or approaches to a problem
  • Answering factual questions about well-documented topics
  • Writing and debugging code
What AI gets wrong — and why

AI tools make mistakes that are easy to miss because they are presented confidently. The main failure modes:

  • Hallucination — AI invents facts, quotes, references, and statistics that do not exist
  • Outdated information — AI training data has a cutoff date and may not know recent events
  • Shallow reasoning — AI can describe arguments without actually evaluating them
  • Confident wrongness — AI does not say "I am not sure". It gives wrong answers in the same tone as correct ones
Critical habit: Never submit AI-generated facts without checking them against a reliable source. Especially names, dates, statistics, and references.

The difference between using AI and letting AI do your work

There is a meaningful difference between these two things:

  • Using AI to learn — asking it to explain something you do not understand, generate practice questions, give feedback on your draft, or help you see a concept from a different angle
  • Using AI instead of learning — asking it to write your essay, answer your exam questions, or produce work you then submit as your own

The first builds your knowledge. The second bypasses it — and often produces work that is generic, inaccurate, and recognisable as AI-generated.

Effective prompting

The quality of what you get from AI depends heavily on how you ask. Vague prompts produce vague answers.

  • Weak prompt: "Tell me about the First World War"
  • Better prompt: "Explain three causes of the First World War as if I am a Year 10 student who already understands nationalism but is confused about the alliance system"

Good prompts include: your level of knowledge, what you already understand, the format you want the response in, and what you want to do with it.

Try this: Add "explain why, not just what" to any factual question. It forces the AI to go deeper and often reveals where its reasoning is shallow.
Specific ways to use AI for revision
  • Explanation on demand: "Explain X in three different ways until one makes sense to me"
  • Practice questions: "Give me five exam-style questions on topic X with mark scheme answers"
  • Feedback on drafts: "Here is my paragraph — what are its strengths and how could it be improved?"
  • Interleaving: "Ask me questions on topics X, Y, and Z in a random order"
  • Simplification: "Summarise this text in bullet points a Year 10 student could understand"

Why referencing AI matters

Academic referencing exists so that readers can check where information came from and give credit to original thinkers. When AI is involved in producing work, the same principles apply — but the rules are still evolving.

The key questions to ask are:

  • Did AI generate ideas or text that appear in my work?
  • Did I use AI to research facts I am now presenting as established?
  • Does my school, college, or teacher require me to declare AI use?
Default position: If in doubt, declare it. Transparency is always safer than concealment.
When AI use is and is not acceptable

This varies by context. Here is a general guide:

  • Usually acceptable: Using AI to explain something you did not understand, to generate practice questions, to get feedback on your own writing, or to help structure your thinking
  • Context-dependent: Using AI to help draft text you then significantly rewrite and make your own — check your school's policy
  • Usually not acceptable: Submitting AI-generated text as your own, using AI to answer assessment questions, copying AI output without acknowledgement

In formal qualifications (GCSEs, A Levels), awarding bodies are increasingly clear that submitting AI-generated work as your own constitutes malpractice.

How to reference AI-generated content

If you have used AI and need to reference it, most citation styles now have guidance. A typical format:

[Tool name] ([Year]). Response to prompt: "[your prompt]". Generated [date]. [Tool URL if applicable].

For example: ChatGPT (2024). Response to prompt: "Explain three causes of the First World War". Generated 14 October 2024. chat.openai.com.

Important: You cannot cite an AI as an authority on a fact. AI references only show you used the tool — you still need a primary or reliable secondary source for any factual claim.

What the evidence says about revision

Not all revision strategies are equal. Cognitive science research consistently shows that some methods feel easy but produce weak memory, while others feel harder but work far better.

  • Passive re-reading and highlighting — feels productive, but is one of the least effective strategies. You are recognising information, not retrieving it.
  • Active retrieval (testing yourself) — one of the most effective strategies. The act of trying to recall information strengthens the memory trace.
  • Spaced practice — spreading revision across time rather than cramming. Returning to a topic after forgetting it forces your brain to reconstruct the memory, making it more durable.
Key idea: The feeling of difficulty during revision is usually a sign the strategy is working. If revision feels easy, you are probably recognising rather than remembering.
Digital tools that use good revision strategies
  • Anki — flashcard app that uses spaced repetition. Cards you struggle with come back sooner. Free and very well evidenced.
  • Quizlet — flashcards with games, tests, and a learn mode. Widely used but check you are testing yourself, not just reading the cards.
  • Seneca Learning — built around retrieval practice and spaced repetition, curriculum-aligned for UK qualifications.
  • Google Forms or Microsoft Forms — create your own practice tests. Making the questions yourself is itself a revision activity.
  • YouTube (carefully) — excellent for seeing concepts explained differently. Mr Bruff, Crash Course, and subject-specific channels can complement your retrieval practice.
A weekly revision routine using digital tools

A structure that puts the evidence into practice:

  • Day 1: Study a topic — read, watch, take notes in OneNote
  • Day 2: Close your notes. Write down everything you remember (brain dump). Check what you missed.
  • Day 4: Test yourself using Anki or Seneca. Focus on the things you got wrong.
  • Day 7: Return to the topic again. Test yourself once more. Update your flashcards if needed.

This pattern — study, retrieve, space, retrieve again — is consistently more effective than an equivalent amount of re-reading time.

TaskBuild a retrieval practice set

Choose one topic you are currently studying in any subject.

  1. Create a set of ten flashcard questions on the topic in Anki, Quizlet, or Microsoft Forms
  2. Test yourself immediately using the set — note your score
  3. Return to the same set in 48 hours and test yourself again without looking at the material first
  4. Compare your two scores. What did you forget? What stuck?

Why digital organisation matters

The average person spends a significant amount of time searching for files they know they have. This is especially costly when you are revising or under pressure with deadlines — you waste time looking for work instead of doing it.

Good digital organisation means you can find anything you need in under 30 seconds. Here is how to build that system.

File naming that actually works

Most students save files with names like "Document1", "history thing", or "final FINAL". This makes search nearly impossible.

A simple naming convention that works:

[Subject] [Topic] [Type] [Date]

  • History_WWI_Essay_Draft1_Oct2024
  • Biology_Photosynthesis_Flashcards_Nov2024
  • English_MacbethNotes_Revision_Jan2025
Key rule: Be specific enough that you know what is in the file before you open it. The date matters because you will have multiple versions over time.
Managing email and calendar effectively

Your school email is not just for receiving messages from teachers — it is a professional communication tool. Habits worth building now:

  • Use folders or labels to organise emails by subject or project
  • Reply to emails within 24 hours where possible
  • Use a clear subject line when you send an email: "Year 10 History — question about coursework deadline" is better than "Hi"

Your school Office 365 account also includes Outlook Calendar. Adding deadlines, exam dates, and revision sessions here means you have one place to see everything that is coming up.

Data is generated constantly

Every digital action generates data. When you use a smartphone, app, or website, data is created about what you did, when, where, and how.

  • Search engines record every search query and the results you click
  • Social media platforms log every post, like, pause, and scroll
  • Mapping apps collect your location continuously
  • Online retailers record every item you view, add to a basket, and buy
  • Even your school systems collect data about your logins, attendance, and submissions
First-party, second-party, and third-party data

Not all data is collected the same way:

  • First-party data — collected directly from you by the service you use. For example, your name and email when you sign up for something.
  • Second-party data — shared between organisations with your (often implicit) consent. For example, when you "sign in with Google".
  • Third-party data — collected by organisations you have no direct relationship with, often through tracking technologies like cookies.
Key idea: You are rarely just a user of a free service. Your data and attention are often what the service is designed to collect.
How data is stored and processed

Once collected, data needs to be stored securely and processed to be useful. Key concepts:

  • Databases — structured stores of data, organised so it can be searched and retrieved efficiently
  • Data processing — applying logic to raw data to produce useful outputs (e.g. recommending a video based on what you have watched)
  • Personal data — any information that can identify you, directly or in combination with other data. Protected in the UK by the UK GDPR.
  • Metadata — data about data. A photo file contains metadata about when and where it was taken, even if the photo itself does not show your face.

Raw data is not useful on its own

Data becomes useful when it is collected with a purpose, cleaned, organised, and analysed to answer a question. Each of these steps matters:

  • Defining the question — what are you actually trying to find out? Vague questions produce useless data.
  • Collecting the data — who or what are you measuring? Is the sample representative?
  • Cleaning the data — removing errors, duplicates, and irrelevant entries
  • Analysing the data — looking for patterns, averages, outliers, and trends
  • Presenting the data — choosing the right chart or format to communicate what you found honestly
How data can mislead

Data can be accurate but still misleading. Common ways this happens:

  • Cherry-picking — showing only the data that supports a conclusion, ignoring contradictory evidence
  • Misleading scales — starting a y-axis at a value other than zero to exaggerate small differences
  • Correlation vs. causation — two things that happen together do not mean one causes the other. Ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer — ice cream does not cause drowning.
  • Small samples — a survey of 12 people is not reliable evidence of what a country thinks
Key habit: When you see a statistic or chart, ask: Who collected this? What was the sample size? What are they not showing you?
Identifying patterns in data

When analysing a dataset, start by looking for:

  • Averages (mean, median, mode) — what is typical? Note that mean can be distorted by extreme values.
  • Range and distribution — how spread out is the data?
  • Outliers — values far from the rest. Are they errors or genuinely interesting?
  • Trends over time — is the data going up, down, or staying stable?
  • Comparisons — how does one group compare to another?

Copyright — the basics

Copyright is an automatic legal right that applies to original creative work as soon as it is created. This includes text, images, music, video, code, and designs.

  • You do not need to register copyright — it exists automatically
  • Copyright typically lasts for the creator's lifetime plus 70 years
  • Using someone else's copyright work without permission is infringement, even if you are not making money from it
  • The fact that something appears on Google Images does not mean it is free to use
Common misconception: "I found it online for free" does not mean you have permission to use it. Free to view is not the same as free to use.
Creative Commons and open licences

Not all creative work is fully protected. Some creators release work under Creative Commons licences that allow specific types of use:

  • CC BY — you can use it if you credit the creator
  • CC BY-SA — you can use and adapt it if you credit the creator and share your version under the same licence
  • CC BY-NC — you can use it for non-commercial purposes if you credit the creator
  • CC0 — no rights reserved. Free to use for anything.

Sites like Unsplash, Pixabay, and Wikimedia Commons offer large libraries of freely licenced images.

How to credit sources properly

Crediting sources is not just about avoiding plagiarism — it is about intellectual honesty and respecting the work of others. A simple format for images:

[Creator name], [title or description], [year], [licence type]. Available at: [URL].

For text sources, use whichever citation format your school uses (Harvard, MLA, APA), applied consistently.

Password security

Weak passwords are one of the most common causes of account compromise. What makes a password weak:

  • Using personal information (name, birthday, pet's name)
  • Using common words or sequences (password, 12345, qwerty)
  • Using the same password across multiple accounts
  • Short passwords — under 12 characters

A strong password is long, random, and unique to each account. A passphrase — four or more random words strung together — is both strong and memorable: correct-horse-battery-staple is far stronger than P@ssw0rd!.

Practical tip: Use a password manager (Bitwarden is free) to generate and store unique passwords for every account. You only need to remember one strong master password.
Phishing and social engineering

Phishing means tricking someone into revealing credentials or clicking a malicious link, usually by impersonating a trusted source.

Red flags to look for in emails and messages:

  • Urgency — "your account will be closed in 24 hours"
  • Generic greetings — "Dear customer" instead of your name
  • Mismatched sender addresses — the display name says "Microsoft" but the email is from a random domain
  • Links that do not match where they claim to go — hover before clicking
  • Requests for passwords, payment, or personal information

Legitimate organisations will never ask for your password by email.

Your digital footprint is increasingly visible to future employers, universities, and anyone who searches for you. Habits worth building now:

  • Review privacy settings on social media annually
  • Think before posting — would this concern a future employer?

What we mean by technology ethics

Technology ethics asks: just because we can do something with technology, should we? And when technology causes harm — who is responsible?

Three frameworks for thinking about ethical questions:

  • Consequentialist: judge actions by their outcomes. Does this technology cause more benefit than harm overall?
  • Rights-based: does this technology violate anyone's fundamental rights — to privacy, to fair treatment, to autonomy?
  • Virtue ethics: what would a good, responsible person or company do in this situation?

Most real ethical questions involve all three, pulling in different directions.

Who is responsible when things go wrong?

When technology causes harm, responsibility is often distributed across multiple actors:

  • Designers and engineers — who built the system and what trade-offs did they make?
  • Companies — what incentives shaped the product decisions?
  • Regulators and governments — what rules existed, and were they adequate?
  • Users — how was the technology used, and was that use foreseeable?
Key tension: Technology companies often argue that their platforms are neutral tools — responsibility lies with users. Critics argue that design choices are never neutral: they shape behaviour, enable harm, and reflect values.

Algorithmic decision-making

  • The right to request human review of an automated decision
  • The right to an explanation of how the decision was made

In practice, these rights are difficult to exercise when systems are proprietary, when explanations are technically complex, or when companies argue the algorithm is a trade secret.

  • Historical bias: training data reflects past inequalities (e.g. hiring models trained on data from when most engineers were male)

  • Measurement bias: using a proxy measure that is easier to collect but less fair (e.g. postcode as a proxy for creditworthiness)

Because AI systems operate at scale and with apparent objectivity, biased outputs can affect many people while being harder to challenge than a human decision.

What the evidence actually says

The relationship between screen time and wellbeing is more complicated than headlines suggest:

  • Total screen time is a poor predictor of wellbeing. What matters more is what you are doing and why
  • Passive consumption (scrolling without purpose) is more consistently associated with lower wellbeing than active use (creating, communicating with close friends)
  • Social comparison — measuring yourself against curated, filtered images of others' lives — is linked to lower self-esteem
  • Disrupted sleep from device use before bed is one of the most robustly evidenced harms
Worth knowing: Many social media platforms are deliberately designed to maximise time on site. Features like infinite scroll, variable reward notifications, and autoplay remove natural stopping points. This is not accidental.
Design for engagement

The apps that dominate your screen time were not designed with your wellbeing in mind — they were designed to maximise engagement, which drives advertising revenue.

Techniques used:

  • Variable reward — unpredictable notifications, like a slot machine, are more compelling than predictable ones
  • Social validation loops — likes, reactions, and follower counts trigger dopamine responses
  • Infinite scroll — removing the natural stopping point of reaching the end
  • Autoplay — the next video starts before you have decided whether to watch it

Understanding these techniques does not make you immune to them — but it changes your relationship to them.

Building a healthier digital life

Practical approaches that the evidence supports:

  • Check screen time statistics weekly and set app limits for platforms that drain time without benefit
  • Keep your phone out of your bedroom or at least across the room when sleeping
  • Turn off all non-essential notifications — the compulsion to check reduces with fewer triggers
  • Audit who you follow: does following this account make you feel better or worse about yourself?
  • Distinguish between use that connects you to people you care about and use that just fills time

Working to a brief

In most professional and creative contexts, you are not making things for yourself — you are making them for someone else, to a specification. A brief defines:

  • The deliverable — what needs to be produced, in what format
  • The audience — who it is for, what they know, what they need
  • The constraints — time, tools, word count, file format, brand guidelines
  • The purpose — what the work should achieve, not just what it should contain

Working to a brief is a discipline. The work is not finished when you are happy with it — it is finished when it meets the brief.

Iteration and improvement

Professional creative work is rarely right first time. Effective digital creators:

  • Produce a rough first version quickly to get feedback on direction before investing time in details
  • Use version history to preserve earlier states they might want to return to
  • Seek specific feedback — "what is unclear?" is more useful than "what do you think?"
  • Distinguish between feedback on execution (how it is made) and feedback on concept (whether the idea is right)
Key principle: Show early and ugly. Getting feedback on a rough draft is more useful than getting feedback on a polished product that has taken you six hours to perfect.
Evaluating your own work honestly

Self-evaluation is a skill that takes practice. Useful questions to ask:

  • Does this meet every point in the brief, or have I unconsciously ignored the parts that were difficult?
  • Would someone from the intended audience understand this without my help?
  • Is there anything here that I included because I like it, but which does not serve the purpose?
  • What is the weakest part, and what would it take to improve it?

What accessibility means in a digital context

Digital accessibility means designing content and products so that people with a range of abilities, disabilities, and circumstances can use them effectively.

It affects more people than most designers assume:

  • Approximately 1 in 5 people in the UK have a disability that affects their use of technology
  • Many more experience temporary situations — a broken arm, bright sunlight on a screen, a slow internet connection
  • Age, language background, and available devices all affect how people experience digital content
Key principle: Accessibility is not a special feature for a minority — it is good design for everyone. Captions on video help people in noisy environments. High contrast helps anyone reading in bright light.
Practical accessibility in documents and presentations
  • Colour contrast: text must have sufficient contrast against its background. Light grey on white fails many users.
  • Alt text: every informational image needs a written description
  • Heading structure: use proper Heading 1, 2, 3 styles — not just large bold text. Screen readers use these to navigate.
  • Readable fonts: sans-serif fonts (Arial, Calibri) at a minimum 12pt for body text
  • Plain language: clear, direct writing helps everyone — especially people with cognitive disabilities, those reading in a second language, or those under time pressure
  • Meaningful link text: "click here" tells a screen reader nothing. "Download the accessibility guide" does.
Assistive technology

Assistive technology is any hardware or software that helps people with disabilities use digital devices:

  • Screen readers — read aloud the content of a screen. Widely used by people with visual impairments. The most popular is JAWS; Windows includes a free built-in screen reader called Narrator.
  • Text-to-speech — converts written text to audio. Used by people with dyslexia, visual impairments, or reading difficulties.
  • Speech recognition — converts spoken words to text. Microsoft Dictate is built into Office 365.
  • Magnification software — enlarges parts of the screen

Understanding assistive technology helps you design content that works with it, not against it.

Your rights under UK GDPR

The UK General Data Protection Regulation gives you specific rights over your personal data held by organisations. The key rights:

  • Right of access: you can request a copy of all personal data an organisation holds about you (a Subject Access Request)
  • Right to rectification: you can ask for inaccurate data to be corrected
  • Right to erasure: in certain circumstances, you can request your data be deleted (sometimes called the 'right to be forgotten')
  • Right to object: you can object to certain types of processing, including direct marketing
  • Right to data portability: in some cases, you can request your data in a format that can be moved to another service
How to exercise your rights

Rights on paper are only valuable if you can use them. In practice:

  • A Subject Access Request (SAR) can be made in writing to any organisation. They must respond within one month.
  • There is no fee for a SAR in most cases
  • If an organisation refuses or delays, you can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) — the UK's data regulator
  • Social media platforms have privacy dashboards where you can download your data
Practical test: Most major platforms allow you to download everything they hold about you. The result is often surprising — location history, every search, every ad you clicked. Try it.
Digital identity and reputation

Your online presence increasingly constitutes a digital identity that follows you into adult life:

  • University admissions processes increasingly involve social media checks
  • Employers routinely search candidates' names before interview
  • Professional platforms like LinkedIn are checked by 90%+ of recruiters

Managing your digital identity means:

  • Knowing what comes up when you search your own name
  • Reviewing privacy settings so only intended audiences see your content
  • Building a positive presence rather than just managing a negative one
  • Understanding what can and cannot be deleted

Technologies shaping the near future

Several technologies are developing rapidly enough to significantly affect daily life and work within the next decade:

Questions to ask about any new technology

The digital literacy skills in this course equip you to approach new technologies critically, regardless of what they are. The questions matter more than the specific answers:

  • Who benefits? Whose interests does this technology primarily serve?
  • Who is at risk? Which groups might be harmed, excluded, or exploited?
  • Who controls it? Who owns the technology, the data it produces, and the decisions it makes?
  • What does it replace? What jobs, relationships, or human activities does it substitute for — and is that a gain or a loss?
  • What could go wrong? What are the failure modes, misuse cases, and unintended consequences?
